Migraine is not "just a headache." It is a neurological event — often preceded by visual auras, nausea, and light sensitivity, and followed by days of fatigue. Ayurveda offers a remarkably clear framework for it: migraine is a disturbance of **Pitta** (heat, inflammation, upward movement) aggravated by **Vata** (the mobile, sharp quality of pain). Understanding your specific trigger pattern is the key to prevention — and Ayurveda is fundamentally a system of prevention.

## The Two Classic Patterns

### Pitta-dominant migraine

- Throbbing, pulsating pain, often on one side.
- Heat, redness, irritability; worse in heat and sun.
- Nausea and acidity.
- Triggered by: skipped meals, excess heat, anger, too much spice/oil, fasting.

### Vata-dominant migraine

- Sharp, shooting, or shifting pain.
- Tension headache spreading from neck and shoulders.
- Worse with stress, travel, irregular routine, and sleep loss.
- Often linked to the menstrual cycle.

## The Most Common Triggers (Know Yours)

1. **Skipped meals or fasting** — blood sugar dips are a leading trigger. The Ayurvedic rule: regular meals at fixed times.
2. **Sleep disturbance** — both too little and irregular sleep.
3. **Dehydration** — and excess caffeine.
4. **Strong smells, bright light, and loud noise.**
5. **Hormonal changes** — the menstrual migraine.
6. **Certain foods** — aged cheese, cured meats, chocolate, and excess caffeine, for some.
7. **Stress and the "let-down" effect** — the weekend migraine that strikes when stress suddenly drops.

## The Ayurvedic Prevention Plan

### 1. Stabilize the Rhythm

- **Regular meals** — never skip; eat at fixed times.
- **Adequate sleep** at consistent hours.
- **Avoid fasting** and crash diets.
- **Hydrate** steadily through the day with warm water.

### 2. Cool the Pitta

- Favor cooling foods: cucumber, bottle gourd, melon, coconut, and coriander.
- Reduce: excess spice, oil, fried food, and fermented food.
- Favor **sweet, bitter, and astringent** tastes over sour and pungent.
- A teaspoon of **Triphala** at night supports regular elimination — constipation is a hidden migraine trigger.

### 3. Calm the Vata

- Warm oil **self-massage** (especially the head, neck, and shoulders) 2–3 times a week.
- A daily **breathing practice** — Anulom Vilom or simple slow breathing.
- Avoid overstimulation: excessive screen time and loud environments.

## Herbs and Remedies Used

- **Brahmi** — calms the mind and supports the nervous system.
- **Jatamansi** — the classical herb for headache and restlessness.
- **Ashwagandha** — supports the stress axis.
- **Tulsi** — a warm tulsi-ginger tea at the first sign of tension.
- **Ginger** — fresh ginger tea can blunt an oncoming attack in some people.
- **Cold compress** on the forehead during a hot, throbbing headache; warm compress for a tight, Vata-type tension headache.
- **Nasya** — a drop of medicated nasal oil (using prepared oils) can help calm the head; a classical practice.

## During the Attack

- **Stop and rest** in a dark, quiet, cool room.
- **Sip warm water** or a mild herbal tea.
- **Cold cloth** on the forehead (Pitta type) or **warm cloth** on the neck (Vata type).
- **Gentle pressure** at the base of the skull and between the brows.
- Do not force yourself to function through the attack — resting early shortens it.

## Panchakarma for Chronic Migraine

For frequent, debilitating migraine, a supervised course of **Shirodhara** — warm medicated oil poured in a steady stream over the forehead — is the classical and often transformative treatment. It calms the nervous system and reduces attack frequency for many patients. **Nasya** and **Virechana** (to clear Pitta) are also used in a physician-guided course.

## When to See a Doctor Immediately

Please seek urgent medical attention if a headache:

- Is sudden and severe ("worst headache of my life").
- Comes with fever, neck stiffness, confusion, or weakness.
- Follows a head injury.
- Is new after age 50 or progressive.
- Comes with vision changes, or you have a history of high blood pressure or a bleeding disorder.

Migraine is manageable — most patients who identify their triggers and follow a consistent rhythm see attacks drop dramatically in frequency within 8–12 weeks. The pattern is the medicine.
