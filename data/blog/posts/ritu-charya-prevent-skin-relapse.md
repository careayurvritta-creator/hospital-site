Anyone with a chronic skin condition knows the calendar almost by heart. The patch returns with the first cold wind, the itch arrives with the monsoon, the redness flares at the height of summer. Ayurveda predicted this rhythm long ago and answered it with **Ritu Charya**, the seasonal routine, a practical system for adjusting food, habits, and care as the year turns.

## Why Seasons Matter to the Skin

Classical texts explain that the doshas rise and fall with the seasons. Heat aggravates **Pitta**, cold and wind disturb **Vata**, and damp heaviness builds **Kapha**. Skin, being the body's outermost tissue, registers these shifts immediately. A person whose Pitta is already high will often flare in hot weather; a Vata-leaning, dry, scaling condition typically worsens in cold, windy months. Ritu Charya works by preparing the body for each shift in advance, so the seasonal swing hits less violently. It is preventive care in the most classical sense.

## The Six Seasons in Classical Terms

The Ayurvedic year is divided into six Ritus, each with its own character.

- **Shishira:** the cold, dewy season, when Kapha accumulates quietly in the body.
- **Vasanta:** spring, when accumulated Kapha tends to mobilize and the skin may show congestion and itching.
- **Grishma:** the hot season, when Pitta rises sharply and inflammatory skin flares become more common.
- **Varsha:** the monsoon, when digestion weakens, humidity rises, and fungal complaints thrive.
- **Sharad:** early autumn, when the accumulated heat of summer surfaces as Pitta-driven skin eruptions.
- **Hemanta:** the cool, comfortable season when digestion strengthens and the body can take richer nourishment.

## Seasonal Swings in Skin Conditions

Once you know the pattern, you can anticipate it.

- **Summer and Sharad:** burning, redness, and inflamed patches often intensify; cooling foods and careful sun habits help.
- **Monsoon:** fungal infections and itching, classically linked to conditions such as **Dadru**, favor warm, dry skin and lighter meals.
- **Winter:** dryness, scaling, and cracking, particularly in Vata-type conditions, respond to oiling and warmth.
- **Spring:** congestion and heaviness can drive Kapha-type eruptions; lighter food and gentle activity keep things moving.

Many patients find that tracking flares against the seasons reveals more than tracking them against any single food.

## Building Your Own Ritu Charya

The seasonal routine is a set of adjustments, not a second career.

1. **Watch the transitions:** the weeks when seasons change are the highest-risk windows; tighten routine and diet during them.
2. **Adjust food with the weather:** favor lighter, cooling meals in hot months, warm nourishing food in winter, and the lightest, driest diet in the monsoon.
3. **Shift daily habits:** warmer oils and fuller moisturizing in cold dry months, and lighter applications in humid ones.
4. **Use seasonal cleansing wisely:** many practitioners plan **Shodhana** (purification) procedures at traditional seasonal junctions, such as spring and autumn.
5. **Keep steady anchors:** fixed sleep, regular meals, and moderate exercise remain constant while everything else flexes.

**Pathya-Apathya**, the guidance on favorable and unfavorable intake, is applied seasonally as well, which is why the same food that suits summer may burden winter.

## The Weeks Between Seasons

Classical texts even address the junction between seasons, the period called Ritu Sandhi, when the body is adjusting and defenses dip. Practical wisdom says keep those weeks simple: eat light, sleep well, avoid travel and excess where possible, and resist the urge to test your skin with new foods or products. Patients who treat these junctions as a quiet zone commonly report fewer surprise flares.

## Who Should Use Care

Seasonal adjustment is gentle, but it is not one-size-fits-all. Your constitution and current condition determine what suits you; a routine that calms one person can aggravate another. Children, pregnant or nursing women, and elderly patients should make significant dietary shifts under guidance. People with chronic conditions such as diabetes or asthma should plan seasonal cleansing only with physician supervision, and no prescribed medication should be stopped because the season changed. If a seasonal flare is severe, infected, or unresponsive to routine care, please see a doctor.

## The Bottom Line

Ritu Charya turns the calendar from a record of past flares into a plan for preventing them. By moving with the seasons rather than against them, many patients find their skin conditions become more predictable and more manageable.
